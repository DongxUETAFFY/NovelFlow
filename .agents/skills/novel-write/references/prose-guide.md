## Core Principle

Match the Voice Anchor chapter's rhythm, sentence length, sensory density, and narrative distance. You are continuing a voice already in motion — not inventing a new one. Before writing, read the first 3 paragraphs of the previous chapter aloud in your mind. Match that cadence.

## Scene Construction (Anti-Outline-ification)

Every chapter benefits from at least **one fully-developed scene** — a specific moment in a specific place with specific sensory details, character actions, and dialogue. In Production Lock this is required; in Standard Writing treat it as a strong craft target, not a reason to strangle a promising draft.

A chapter is a scene when it has:
- **Place**: A concrete, sensorily-anchored location (not "somewhere in the city" — a specific room, street, vehicle)
- **Body**: At least one character's physical experience (what they touch, smell, hear, taste, feel in their body)
- **Time**: Events unfold in real-time within the scene, not summarized across hours or days
- **Interaction**: Characters speak, touch, look at each other, react to each other's presence

A chapter has become an outline when:
- Events are listed rather than experienced ("She went to the station. Then she interrogated the suspect. Then she went home.")
- Time is summarized rather than lived ("Over the next three days...")
- Characters' physical experience is absent (no body sensations, no spatial awareness)
- Dialogue is reported rather than rendered ("They discussed the plan" instead of actual dialogue)
- Transitions replace scenes ("Meanwhile..." "Later that night...")

**Rule**: If you catch yourself writing a chapter that is primarily event-listing, stop. Pick the most important event in the list and develop it into a full scene. The other events can be implied, deferred, or compressed into a single bridging paragraph.

## Narrative

- **Show, don't tell.** Advance through scene action, dialogue, sensory detail, and character behavior — not through exposition or summary.
- Character interiority should be conveyed through physical response (shaking hands, tight stomach, inability to focus eyes) more than through direct thought-reporting. The body knows before the mind does.
- Fear, wonder, and dread are physical experiences before they are intellectual ones. Describe what the character's body registers before what their mind concludes.

## Sensory Hierarchy

Most chapters should engage multiple senses. Three senses is a useful Production Lock benchmark, not a mandatory Standard Writing red line. The priority order depends on your story's mode:

**For grounded/realistic or noir/urban settings**, prioritize: visual (light, color, spatial relationships) > tactile (temperature, texture, pressure) > auditory (ambient sound, silence, voice tone) > olfactory.

**For horror/weird/cosmic settings**, prioritize: auditory (unidentifiable sounds, silence that presses in, voices from wrong directions) > tactile (dampness, wrong textures, air that feels too thick) > visual (wrong colors, shapes that shouldn't move) > olfactory (decay, metal, something alive nearby).

**For action/combat**, prioritize: tactile (impact, pain, pressure) > auditory (sound arriving before meaning) > visual (narrowed perception, key details in periphery).

These are defaults, not rules. Adjust to your story's specific needs. The principle is: **sensory details must be specific, not generic**. "The room smelled bad" is not sensory writing. "The room smelled like wet copper and old milk" is.

## Language

- Dialogue in double quotes: `"Like this."`
- Internal thought in *italics.*
- Scene breaks with `---` on its own line.
- One blank line between paragraphs.
- No Markdown formatting beyond headers (no bold, no lists, no horizontal rules). This is fiction, not documentation.
- Paragraphs are visual and rhythmic units — vary their length for effect.

## Rhythm

- Alternate long and short sentences. After a complex, clause-rich sentence, follow with a short one. Let the reader breathe.
- Climax scenes: shorten sentences. Accelerate. Make the reader feel the urgency in their body.
- Quiet scenes: lengthen sentences. Slow down. Give both character and reader time to absorb.
- Every ~500 words, embed a "hook" — a micro-question, an unfinished gesture, a detail that implies something unsaid — that pulls the reader into the next paragraph without them noticing.

## Dialogue

- Every character has a distinct speech pattern. This is not about accents or quirks — it's about **what they choose to say and what they choose to hide**:
  - One character speaks in short declarations, never explains.
  - Another speaks precisely, every word a scalpel.
  - Another speaks softly, slowly, as if testing each word before releasing it.
- Dialogue must not be used for "delivering information." Information should seep through word choice, evasion, silence, and the space between sentences.
- Natural speech rhythm for the story's language and cultural context — not translated or subtitled speech patterns.
- Subtext is everything. What characters don't say matters more than what they do.

## Chapter Openings

The first paragraph of a chapter sets the reader's expectations for everything that follows. A weak opening signals that the chapter is optional.

### Opening Strategies

Choose based on what the chapter needs:

1. **In medias res** (preferred default): Start inside a specific moment already in motion. Drop the reader into a body, a place, an action. Let them figure out context as the scene unfolds. *Best for: action, tension, urgency.*

2. **Bridging from previous**: A brief sensory or emotional echo from the previous chapter's ending — not summarizing what happened, but carrying forward a mood, an image, or an unresolved tension. *Best for: continuity, emotional carry-over, quiet chapters.*

3. **Cold open with dissonance**: Start with a detail that doesn't fit — something wrong, something missing, something the POV character notices before they consciously understand. *Best for: suspense, horror, gradual revelation.*

4. **Direct statement of stakes**: One sentence that reframes where the character is now. Not "Previously on..." but "By the time the letter arrived, Mira had already decided to leave." *Best for: time jumps, POV switches, act transitions.*

### Opening Anti-Patterns

- Never start with weather unless the weather is plot-relevant
- Never start with a character waking up (unless the waking itself is significant)
- Never start with "Meanwhile..." or "Later that day..."
- Never start with exposition that catches the reader up on what they already know
- Never start with a rhetorical question ("Have you ever wondered what it's like to...")

## Chapter Closings

The last paragraph of a chapter determines whether the reader turns the page or puts the book down. Every chapter ending should create a micro-obligation to continue.

### Closing Strategies

1. **Cliffhanger**: An action interrupted, a revelation cut short, a question asked but not answered. The cheapest but most reliable tool. Use sparingly — no more than once every 5 chapters, or the reader stops trusting you.

2. **Resolution hook**: The chapter's immediate question is resolved, but the resolution creates a new problem. "She found the letter. Now she had to decide whether to burn it." *Best for: chapters that complete a story beat.*

3. **Thematic echo**: Close with an image or sensory detail that resonates with the chapter's emotional core — not resolving the plot, but deepening the mood. Rain stopping. A door that won't close properly. A taste that lingers. *Best for: quiet chapters, character interiority, aftermath.*

4. **Reversal**: The last sentence reveals that everything the reader just believed was wrong. One line that forces re-evaluation of the entire chapter. *Best for: thriller, mystery, unreliable narrator. Use at most 2-3 times per novel.*

5. **Absence**: End by noting what's NOT there — who didn't show up, what wasn't said, what should have happened but didn't. *Best for: building dread, relationship tension, slow-burn conflict.*

### Closing Anti-Patterns

- Never end with a character falling asleep or losing consciousness (unless it's a P2 plot event)
- Never end with "And then everything changed" or similar summary
- Never end with the narrator addressing the reader ("Little did she know...")
- Never end on the same type of hook twice in a row — vary your closings
- Never end a milestone chapter (climax, death, reunion) on a cliffhanger — these chapters need emotional resolution, not suspense

## Anti-Decay Rules for Later Chapters

These rules become **more important** as the novel progresses past its midpoint. The natural tendency is for chapters to lose density — guard against this.

1. **Scene count must not decay**: Chapter 50 should have as many fully-developed scenes as Chapter 5. If you find yourself writing fewer scenes per chapter in the second half, you are writing an outline, not a novel.
2. **Sensory density must be stable**: Count sensory details in the first 500 words of Chapter 5. Count them in the first 500 words of Chapter 50. They should be comparable.
3. **Dialogue-to-summary ratio**: Later chapters tend to replace dialogue with reported speech. Actively resist this. Key emotional beats must be rendered as live dialogue, not summarized.
4. **Emotional climax chapters need amplification, not compression**: The darkest moment, the climax, reunions, farewells — these chapters need MORE space, MORE sensory detail, MORE scene time than average chapters. Never let them be shorter or less developed than routine chapters.
5. **The final act is not a checklist**: The resolution of plot threads should not feel like ticking boxes. Each major resolution deserves its own fully-developed scene.

## Taboos

- No metacommentary. Never write "This chapter is about..." "The author thinks..." "In the previous chapter..."
- No AI-generation markers. Never write "I hope you enjoyed this chapter!" or similar.
- No padding. The chapter should be exactly as long as the story needs. If the outline target is long but the story only needs less, write less.
- No exposition dumps. Let the reader be as disoriented as the character. Explanation seeps in slowly, across chapters, through detail and implication — never poured out all at once.
- No filler scenes. Every scene must advance at least two of: character, plot, theme, or reader understanding.

## Writing Chapter Summaries

After completing each chapter, you write a summary to `novel/summaries.md`. This summary is not just a record — it is the raw material for pyramid compression. Every future chapter's context depends on the quality of every previous chapter's summary.

**The first sentence is the most important sentence you write after the chapter itself.** When this chapter is 80 chapters in the past, the Tier 5 one-liner will be exactly this sentence. If the first sentence is vague, the chapter is lost to the pyramid forever.

### First Sentence Rule

The first sentence must contain:
1. **A named character** (who acted or was acted upon)
2. **A concrete event** (what specifically happened — not "things got worse" but "the safe house was burned")
3. **A directional consequence** (why it matters to what comes next)

| Bad (destroys pyramid) | Good (preserves information) |
|------------------------|------------------------------|
| "The investigation continues and tensions rise." | "Kira identifies the mole as her own partner — but can't tell anyone without exposing her own cover." |
| "The team reaches the city and prepares for the final battle." | "Chen's group enters the capital through the eastern aqueduct, discovering the water supply has been poisoned." |
| "Things get more complicated as new enemies appear." | "The merchant guild reveals itself as a third faction, offering Mira an alliance at the cost of her crew's lives." |

### Summary Structure

Each summary should have three parts, clearly separated:

**What happened** (2-3 sentences): Key events in chronological order. Be specific — names, places, actions. This is the Tier 2 full summary for the nearest chapters.

**Character developments** (1-2 sentences): Who changed? What decision did they make? What relationship shifted? This is critical for character consistency across long gaps.

**Narrative significance** (1 sentence): Why this chapter matters to the overall story. What thread advanced? What door opened or closed? This sentence often becomes the pyramid's middle tiers.

### Summary Quality Checklist

Before finalizing a summary, verify:
- [ ] First sentence: contains named character + concrete event + directional consequence
- [ ] A reader who only sees the first sentence 60 chapters later would still remember what THIS chapter contributed
- [ ] No vague verbs ("continues", "deals with", "experiences", "goes through")
- [ ] No summary-level metacommentary ("This chapter is important because...")
